Why This Work Matters to Me

On 13th and what archival materials can do

There is a moment in Ava DuVernay's 13th where she cuts from photographs of Black families after the Civil War — ordinary mothers, fathers, sons, and daughters — to images of Black men and boys in prison uniforms performing labor. I had heard the history. I knew what happened. But seeing those specific images placed next to each other, the deliberate choice to show humanity and then immediately show how it was stripped away, that is something else entirely.

Someone chose those images. Someone researched and cleared and organized those materials and understood what was at stake in how they were used. That is the work I want to learn to do — not because it is glamorous, but because it is foundational to doing this right. That deliberate discomfort is what great documentary does, and it is what I want to carry into my work with archival materials.

A Point of View — Two Versions of the Same Scene

On perspective and what it means for archival work

For a music supervision assignment, I was asked to re-supervise a scene from Waiting to Exhale — first with my own music choice, then with an AI-suggested alternative. I edited both versions. The choices about what you hear are mine in each case.

What the two versions reveal is not just a difference in taste. They reveal a difference in perspective. Both tracks express hurt and rage. But a Black woman's hurt and rage, expressed through the lens of another Black woman, sounds different than when expressed through a lens that does not carry that specific history. That difference lives in the selection — and it lives in what the person making the decision brings to the room.

Archival work carries the same responsibility. The materials you choose, the context you provide, the framing you build around what you found — these are not neutral decisions. Who is making them matters. I want to be in that room with that awareness.
