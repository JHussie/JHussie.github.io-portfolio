A Point of View — Two Versions of the Same Scene

The question underneath the work

For a music supervision assignment, I was asked to re-supervise a scene from Waiting to Exhale — first with my own music choice, then with an AI-suggested alternative. I edited both versions. The choices about what you hear are mine in each case.

What the two versions reveal is not just a difference in taste. They reveal a difference in perspective. Both tracks express hurt and rage. But a Black woman's hurt and rage, expressed through the lens of another Black woman, sounds different than when expressed through a lens that does not carry that specific history. That difference lives in the music selection, and it lives in what the music supervisor — and the director, and the music editor — brings to the room.

Music supervision is a cultural act. Who is making the decision shapes what gets expressed and how. I want to be in that room with that awareness.

Version A
My selection
The rage here has texture and history. It knows exactly what it is and where it comes from. The sonic choice carries that specificity.
Version B
AI-suggested, my edit
The rage here is equally real but more generalized. It reaches for a universal emotional register rather than a specific cultural one. Neither version is wrong. But they are not the same.
What this means in practice

When a brand asks for music that feels "emotional" or "authentic" or "culturally relevant," the music supervisor is the person in the room who has to know the difference — and be able to argue for it with specificity. That is a skill built from genuine engagement with music across cultures and contexts, not just familiarity with a catalog.
